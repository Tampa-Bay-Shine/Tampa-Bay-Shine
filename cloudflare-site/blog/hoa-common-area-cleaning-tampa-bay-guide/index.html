HOA & Condo Common Area Cleaning in Tampa Bay: A Property Manager's Guide

Build a practical cleaning scope for lobbies, hallways, elevators, mailrooms, clubhouses and other shared community spaces.

Quick answer

A good HOA or condo common-area cleaning plan is built around resident traffic and property zones. Define the frequency for lobbies, hallways, elevators, mailrooms, clubhouses, fitness areas, restrooms and other shared spaces separately, then use inspections and a communication process to keep standards consistent.

Treat each shared space as its own cleaning zone

ZoneCommon concerns
Lobby and entrancesFirst impression, glass, tracked-in soil and high-touch surfaces
Hallways and corridorsFloor appearance, dust, corners and resident traffic
ElevatorsFloors, doors, tracks, mirrors and touchpoints
Mail/package areasTraffic, debris and frequently touched surfaces
Clubhouse/amenitiesTables, counters, floors, restrooms and event-related use
Fitness areasFloors, mirrors and facility-defined equipment/surface responsibilities

Frequency should follow traffic, not just square footage

A compact lobby used by hundreds of residents can need more attention than a much larger low-traffic room. Property managers should look at entrances, elevators, mail areas and amenity spaces at different times of day to understand where appearance degrades first.

Seasonal events, move-ins, move-outs, community events and construction activity can temporarily change the workload. Build a process for requesting additional service rather than expecting a routine checklist to absorb every unusual event.

Write a scope that can actually be inspected

“Clean common areas” is too vague for reliable quality control. Specify zones, tasks and frequency. Identify who handles trash, glass, restrooms, consumable supplies, interior windows, floor detail work and periodic projects. If a task is important enough to generate a complaint, it should be clear who owns it.

Use simple quality control and communication

Property managers need a predictable way to report an issue without rebuilding the entire cleaning plan. A useful system includes a primary contact, a defined response path, periodic walkthroughs and clear differentiation between routine service, corrective work and separately quoted projects.

Questions for a common-area cleaning proposal

  1. Is the proposal organized by area and frequency?
  2. Are amenity rooms and restrooms clearly included or excluded?
  3. Who handles consumable supplies?
  4. How are special events or extra visits requested?
  5. How are quality issues documented and resolved?
  6. Can the vendor provide a COI when the association or management company requires one?

Frequently asked questions

Should every common area be cleaned at the same frequency?

No. Entrances, elevators and mail areas often experience a different level of traffic than meeting rooms or lower-use amenities.

What about periodic floor work or windows?

Those services may need a separate frequency and scope. They should be scheduled explicitly rather than assumed to be part of routine janitorial visits.

How can an HOA reduce cleaning complaints?

Use an inspectable scope, match frequency to actual traffic and give management and the cleaning provider a clear communication path.
